## ACT I — The Sunday Dread & The Peasant Shock

```text
Every Sunday night, right around seven or eight o'clock... you feel it, don't you? You're still off work. You're sitting on your own couch. Nobody's asking you for a spreadsheet. And yet — your chest tightens up just a little bit. [sighs] Because tomorrow is Monday. And Monday means five straight days of handing over your life... just to earn back two days at the end. Five days on. Two days off.

We're so used to it that we treat it like a law of nature. Like seven days in a week, or twenty-four hours in a day. And if you ask people WHY we work like this, everyone gives you the exact same answer. "Well, life used to be way worse! People worked from sunrise to sunset every single day just to survive. The five-day week is the best deal humans have ever had."

That's the official story. [chuckles] There's just one tiny problem with it. It's completely backwards.

When historians actually dug into old tax ledgers, church calendars, and court records from before factories existed... they found the exact OPPOSITE. Back in 1991, Harvard economist Juliet Schor went through the work records of 14th-century English peasants. Think about who these people were. No tractors. No electricity. No labor laws. No unions. And yet? In a normal year, a medieval peasant worked about ONE hundred and fifty days.

[surprised] Yeah. You heard that right. Not two hundred and sixty days like a modern office worker. One hundred and fifty.

Between winter slowdowns, harvest breaks, and over a HUNDRED church feast days, medieval peasants spent almost HALF the year not working for their lord at all. Even on workdays, they stopped for long lunches, afternoon naps, and ale breaks. Wait — think about that for a second. We have power grids, laptops, and machines built to save time... so HOW did we end up in a stricter schedule than a 14th-century peasant?!

To figure that out, we have to look at a 300-year fight over something sitting in your pocket right now... the clock.
```

## ACT II — Saint Monday & The Steam Engine Cage

```text
For almost all of human history, people didn't work by the hour. They worked by the TASK. If you were a cobbler, a blacksmith, or a weaver in the 1700s, you worked in bursts. An order came in? You went hard. Harvest was ready? You got it done. And the second the job was finished... you stopped. You went home.

In fact, workers across England, France, and early America had a weekly habit that drove business owners completely insane. [chuckles] They called it "Saint Monday."

Sunday was for church, family, and the tavern. Which meant Monday was for... recovering from Sunday. Workshops were half-empty every Monday. Plenty of weavers didn't even TOUCH their looms until Tuesday afternoon — they'd just work late on Thursday and Friday to hit their quota. You owned your own time.

...Until the steam engine showed up and ruined everything.

See, a wooden handloom can sit in the corner all Monday morning and it costs ZERO dollars. A giant coal-powered factory? Not so much. Every hour those iron machines sat cold was money burning straight up the chimney. As historian E. P. Thompson wrote back in 1967, the Industrial Revolution wasn't just about machines. It was about breaking human habits.

Factory owners didn't want artisans. They wanted human gears — showing up at the exact same minute, six days a week, fifty-two weeks a year. In lots of early textile mills, workers weren't even ALLOWED to carry pocket watches. [whispers] Seriously. Only the master clock on the factory wall could tell time — and owners would actually slow it down during the shift so workers stayed an extra thirty minutes without ever knowing.

Saint Monday got crushed with brutal fines. Those hundred feast days? Wiped right off the calendar. By the 1850s, the new normal across Europe and America was six full days a week, twelve hours a day — with only Sunday left to collapse. Humans didn't evolve to work like this. We got shoved into a six-day cage in a SINGLE generation.
```

## ACT III — Henry Ford's 1926 Secret

```text
So how did we ever get Saturday back? Open any textbook and they'll hand all the credit to one guy: Henry Ford. On September 25th, 1926, Ford shocked the entire business world. He announced his factory workers were dropping down to FIVE days a week — Monday to Friday — for the exact same six-dollar daily pay.

[sarcastically] Wow. What a saint, right? Other bosses lost their minds. The head of U.S. Steel actually warned that giving workers two whole days off would destroy American morals and turn everyone into lazy criminals.

Wait — hold on. Stop right there. Henry Ford was one of the most ruthless control freaks in business history. The guy literally had a private spy force checking up on his workers so nobody slacked off. He did NOT give away millions of hours of labor because he felt bad for you.

He just ran into a math problem.

By the mid-1920s, Ford's assembly lines were pumping out Model T cars faster than ever. His workers even made enough money to buy one. There was just one catch. WHY would a guy buy a family car if he worked six days a week, passed out on Saturday night, and spent his only day off sitting in church on Sunday?! A worker who's always working has ZERO time to be a customer.

Listen to what Ford himself told World's Work magazine in 1926, right after the announcement: "People who have more leisure must have more clothes. They must have a greater variety of food. They must have more transportation facilities. This increased consumption will require greater production than we now have."

[softly] Read that again. Saturday wasn't given back so you could relax under a tree like an ancient hunter-gatherer. Saturday was invented so you'd have time to go SHOPPING. One day to rest on Sunday. One day to spend your paycheck on Saturday. [sighs] And five days to earn it all back again.
```

## ACT IV — The Forgotten 1933 30-Hour Bill

```text
Now, here's the part of the story they definitely skipped in school. Back in the 1930s, NOBODY thought the 40-hour week was the finish line. Everyone assumed it was just a quick pit stop on the way down. In 1930, famous economist John Maynard Keynes ran the numbers on where technology was heading and predicted his grandkids — basically us — would only work about FIFTEEN hours a week. Like, three hours a day.

And he wasn't some fringe dreamer.

In April 1933, right in the middle of the Great Depression, the U.S. Senate actually voted on a bill from Senator Hugo Black. It would've made it ILLEGAL for most factories and businesses to work anyone more than THIRTY hours a week. Six hours a day, five days a week. [excited] And it PASSED the U.S. Senate — fifty-three to thirty!

Kellogg's even switched their Battle Creek cereal factory to six-hour shifts — and guess what happened? Accidents dropped, efficiency went UP, and workers got off at two in the afternoon to actually hang out with their kids. For a few wild months in 1933, the six-hour workday was inches away from becoming American law.

Then the corporate lobbyists swarmed Washington. Business leaders warned President Roosevelt that if workers ever got a TASTE of a 30-hour week, they'd never go back to 40. So the bill got quietly buried in the House. Five years later, in 1938, the Fair Labor Standards Act locked the law at forty hours across five days.

And then... the clock just froze.
```

## ACT V — Parkinson's Law & The 90-Year Experiment

```text
Since 1938, American worker productivity has jumped by over THREE HUNDRED percent. A job that took a whole room of clerks an entire week in 1940 now takes one spreadsheet... FOUR seconds. By every law of math, Keynes should've been right. We should all be logging off on Tuesday afternoon.

So WHY are you still staring at your laptop at 5:01 on a Friday?!

In 1955, a British historian named Cyril Northcote Parkinson nailed it in one line: "Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion." When we moved from factory floors to office cubicles, companies stopped measuring what you actually BUILT — and kept measuring how many hours your butt sat in a chair. Give a five-hour job forty hours of office time, and it magically sprouts meetings, status reports, and endless email threads until it eats all forty hours.

Anthropologist David Graeber found that millions of office workers privately admit they finish their REAL work in fifteen to twenty hours a week — almost the exact same seventeen hours recorded among Kalahari hunter-gatherers. [sighs] The other twenty hours? Just acting busy.

And all that extra wealth our machines DID create? Instead of trading it for Tuesday and Wednesday off, we got sold on trading it for bigger houses, newer phones, and endless subscriptions — stuff that takes a five-day paycheck just to keep paying for.

The five-day workweek isn't some ancient law of humanity. It's barely ninety years old. Born from steam engines, tweaked by a carmaker who needed weekend shoppers, and frozen in 1938.

[softly] Which makes that tight feeling in your chest on Sunday night make a lot more sense. You're not broken for hating a forty-hour fluorescent box. Your body just remembers how human time is supposed to feel.
```
