## Verbatim Final Spoken Narration

Look closely at the machine that launched modern rocketry in nineteen twenty-six. It had no outer skin and no nose cone—just a tiny nozzle pulling two fuel tanks on naked metal pipes.

Thirty years later, early orbital rockets still looked like fragile metal pencils. In nineteen fifty-seven, Vanguard lifted four feet before collapsing into flames. Weeks later, Juno One reached orbit by spinning an exposed tub of eleven miniature rocket tubes bolted to its nose.

By nineteen sixty-two, NASA's Mercury-Atlas gleamed like solid steel—yet its tank walls were thinner than a dime. Left unpressurized, the entire rocket would crumple under its own weight, dropping two side boosters mid-sky as it climbed.

Mid-sixties rockets left raw machinery wide open. Gemini's Titan Two fired twin engines inside an exposed steel frame. And for Saturn One-B in nineteen sixty-six, NASA bolted eight narrow missile tanks in a circle—above eight tightly packed engines.

Then nineteen sixty-nine unveiled a thirty-six-story colossus: Saturn Five. Beneath its hull hung five cavernous F-1 nozzles—each nearly four meters wide in rough silver batting—unleashing seven point five million pounds of thrust as ice cascaded down its sides.

In nineteen eighty-one, rocket geometry broke symmetry completely. The Space Shuttle mounted a black ceramic-tiled glider beside a giant orange tank, firing three computer-throttled hydrogen engines glowing with blue shock diamonds.

Physics still forces propellant to fill nearly ninety percent of every rocket's mass. Yet today, NASA's Artemis super-rocket fuses four laser-upgraded RS-25 engines with twin boosters—unleashing eight point eight million pounds of thrust back toward the Moon.
