## YouTube Description (Ready to Copy-Paste — Includes Required Media Credits)

In March 1926, Robert H. Goddard launched the world's first liquid-fueled rocket ("Nell") in Auburn, Massachusetts: a bare 10-foot frame with no outer aerodynamic skin and no nose cone, pulling two fuel tanks beneath an exposed top nozzle on naked metal pipes.

Over the next century, rocket hardware transformed decade by decade through trial, failure, and radical structural architecture:
• 1957–1958: Slender orbital "metal pencils" — Vanguard TV-3 collapsing into flames on Cape Canaveral Pad 18A, followed weeks later by Juno I spinning an open tub of 11 scaled-down solid rocket tubes to place Explorer 1 in orbit.
• 1962: NASA's gleaming stainless-steel Mercury-Atlas "balloon tank" whose walls (<1 mm, thinner than a dime) would crumple under their own weight if depressurized, staging 1.5-stage side boosters mid-climb.
• 1965–1966: Open-truss Titan II interstages for Gemini and Saturn IB's cluster of eight narrow Redstone/Jupiter tanks bolted in a circle above eight H-1 engines.
• 1969: The 36-story Saturn V firing five cavernous F-1 nozzles wrapped in silver thermal batting (7.5 million lbf thrust) as cryogenic ice cascaded down its hull.
• 1981: Asymmetric Space Shuttle architecture pairing a black ceramic-tiled winged glider beside an external liquid-hydrogen/oxygen tank and three computer-throttled RS-25 main engines glowing with Mach shock diamonds.
• Today: Governed by the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation (~90% propellant mass fraction), NASA's Space Launch System (Artemis) pairs four upgraded RS-25 engines with twin solid rocket boosters for 8.8 million pounds of thrust back toward the Moon.
