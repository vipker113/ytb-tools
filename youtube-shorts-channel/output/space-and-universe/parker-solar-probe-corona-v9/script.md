## Verbatim Final Spoken Narration

How did a NASA spacecraft fly straight through the Sun's million-degree atmosphere—without melting?

Launched atop a Delta IV Heavy rocket, Parker Solar Probe plunged within three point eight million miles of the Sun. Its onboard camera filmed glowing plasma streamers rushing directly past the spacecraft.

Out in the solar corona, particles race past a million degrees Celsius—yet the gas is so thin that very few strike the probe. Temperature measures particle speed, not total heat transfer.

A four-point-five-inch carbon-foam shield reflects blinding sunlight, peaking near thirteen hundred seventy degrees Celsius while instruments behind stay near thirty degrees.

Physicists still debate which magnetic waves superheat the outer corona hundreds of times hotter than the solar surface. Yet when flight controllers heard Parker's survival signal, humanity had touched a star—and survived.

## Factual Sources Supporting the Narration

1. **Kasper, J. C., Klein, K. G., Lichko, E., et al. (2021). Parker Solar Probe Enters the Magnetically Dominated Solar Corona. Physical Review Letters, 127(25), 255101.** — https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.127.255101
   - *Supports:* Confirmed humanity's first direct spacecraft entry below the Alfvén critical surface into the magnetically dominated solar corona (>1,000,000°C ultra-tenuous plasma vs ~5,500°C photosphere) and ongoing investigation of magnetic switchbacks and Alfvénic wave heating.
2. **NASA Goddard Space Flight Center & Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory (Congdon, B. et al., 2018; Dec 2024 Perihelion Fact Sheet). Traveling to the Sun: Why Won't Parker Solar Probe Melt?** — https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/heliophysics/traveling-to-the-sun-why-wont-parker-solar-probe-melt/
   - *Supports:* At 3.8 million miles (6.1 million km) closest solar approach, low coronal particle density limits conductive heat transfer while Parker's 4.5-inch (11.4 cm), 97%-void alumina-coated carbon-composite foam Thermal Protection System endures up to ~1,370°C (2,500°F) on its sun-facing side and keeps spacecraft instruments near ~30°C (85°F).
