## Verbatim Final Spoken Narration

NASA's Kepler space telescope revealed over three hundred million rocky habitable worlds across our galaxy. Billions of older stars formed long before our Sun ignited.

Even crawling at one percent of light speed, a starship fleet crosses the galaxy in ten million years. In cosmic time, that's a blink—so physicist Enrico Fermi asked: where is everybody?

Since nineteen sixty, giant radio dishes and optical telescopes have scanned thousands of nearby star systems. Across more than sixty years of listening, confirmed alien signals equal zero.

To explain why alien life feels so rare, Robin Hanson proposed the Great Filter. Between dead rock and starflight sits at least one brutal barrier almost nothing survives.

If that filter lies behind us, we already hit the cosmic jackpot. On early Earth, simple microbes stalled for nearly two billion years before two cells merged into complex life.

The darker option puts the Great Filter ahead of us. Perhaps rocky worlds sprout intelligent species often—only to wipe themselves out once nuclear or climate tech arrives.

Still, radio silence doesn't prove we're alone: astronomers calculate our searches have sampled barely a glass from the cosmic ocean.

Until one signal breaks the quiet, Earth remains the only voice we know. Tonight, our telescopes keep listening across three hundred million silent worlds.

## Factual Sources Supporting the Narration

1. **Bryson, S., Kunimoto, M., Kopparapu, R. K., Coughlin, J. L., Borucki, W. J., et al. (2021). The Occurrence of Rocky Habitable-Zone Planets around Solar-Like Stars from Kepler Data. The Astronomical Journal, 161(1), 36.** (DOI: `10.3847/1538-3881/abc418`) — https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-3881/abc418
   - *Supports:* NASA Kepler occurrence-rate analysis showing sun-like stars commonly host rocky habitable-zone exoplanets (~300 million+ potentially habitable rocky worlds across the Milky Way).
2. **Hart, M. H. (1975). An Explanation for the Absence of Extraterrestrials on Earth. Quarterly Journal of the Royal Astronomical Society, 16, 128–135; & Jones, E. M. (1985). 'Where is everybody?' An account of Fermi's question. Los Alamos National Laboratory Report LA-10311-MS.** — https://fas.org/irp/agency/dod/jason/fermi.pdf
   - *Supports:* Enrico Fermi's 1950 Los Alamos question ('Where is everybody?') and galactic crossing timescales (~10 million years even at sub-relativistic ~1% c starflight speeds); zero confirmed extraterrestrial signals since Project Ozma in 1960.
3. **Hanson, R. (1998). The Great Filter — Are We Almost Past It? George Mason University / Future of Humanity Research Paper.** — https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/greatfilter.html
   - *Supports:* The Great Filter framework: an extreme evolutionary or technological barrier between dead matter and galaxy-spanning civilization, located either behind us (e.g., Earth's ~2-billion-year prokaryote-to-eukaryote transition) or ahead of us (technological self-destruction).
4. **Wright, J. T., Kanodia, S., & Lubar, E. (2018). How Much SETI Has Been Done? Finding Needles in the n-Dimensional Cosmic Haystack. The Astronomical Journal, 156(6), 260.** (DOI: `10.3847/1538-3881/aae099`) — https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-3881/aae099
   - *Supports:* Quantitative nine-dimensional SETI search-volume calculation demonstrating that all radio/optical SETI surveys to date have sampled only a minute fraction (comparable to a glass/hot-tub of water out of Earth's oceans), meaning silence does not prove we are alone.
