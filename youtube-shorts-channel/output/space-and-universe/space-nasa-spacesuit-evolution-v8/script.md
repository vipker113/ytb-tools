## Verbatim Final Spoken Narration

Look closely at America's first spacesuit in nineteen sixty-one. Wrapped in mirror-bright aluminized nylon, it was only an emergency pressure cocoon. Inflated in vacuum, its fabric joints locked so stiff an astronaut could barely bend an elbow.

Four years later, Project Gemini pushed astronauts outside into raw vacuum. Tethered by a twenty-five-foot golden umbilical hose, Ed White floated over Earth firing a handheld gas gun. Yet internal pressure ballooned soft Gemini suits like car tires, nearly exhausting crews squeezing back inside.

Walking on the Moon in nineteen sixty-nine demanded a wearable twenty-one-layer spacecraft. Beneath fireproof white Beta-cloth, stacked Mylar and ballistic armor stopped tiny space rocks at eight kilometers per second. A thin gold-plated visor reflected blinding, unfiltered sunlight.

On each astronaut's back rode a life-support pack pumping chilled water through tubes against their skin. Still, Apollo's pressurized fabric torso fought every bend—forcing crews to bunny-hop and topple whenever they reached down for rocks.

In nineteen eighty-four, NASA rebuilt the upper body around a rigid fiberglass shell. Snapped onto a twenty-four-thruster nitrogen jetpack, Bruce McCandless flew untethered one hundred meters from Challenger. He became a standalone human satellite above Earth.

Now for twenty twenty-six, Artemis lunar suits replace stiff fabric bends with sealed metal bearings. Astronauts step straight through a rear-entry hatch on the backpack, then twist and kneel effortlessly across rocky craters.

Glass-sharp lunar dust remains NASA's toughest enemy, chewing at metal seals and joints. Yet sixty-five years after Mercury's silver cocoon, dust-hardened Artemis suits are built to work deep inside the Moon's frozen southern shadows.
