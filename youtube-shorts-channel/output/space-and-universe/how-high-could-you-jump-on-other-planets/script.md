- **Physics assumption (shown on screen):** the *same takeoff speed* on every world, equal to a `0.50 m` standing jump on Earth (`v0 = 3.13 m/s`). Height `h = v0²/(2g) = 0.50 m ÷ (g/g_Earth)`; airtime `T = 2·v0/g`. This ignores spacesuits, air resistance, and how leg muscles would actually perform under different body weights.
- **Why `0.50 m`:** a round, illustrative baseline (typical untrained adults jump roughly `0.30–0.45 m`; trained athletes `0.5–0.8 m`). The narration presents it as a premise ("Say you manage half a meter"), not as an average.

## Final Verbatim Spoken Narration (word-for-word match to `narration.mp3`)

How high could you jump on other planets? Say you manage half a meter on Earth.

On Mars, the same jump reaches about 1.3 meters. Tiny Mercury? Almost the same.

On the Moon, you'd rise 3 meters and float for nearly four seconds.

On Jupiter, from a platform in its clouds, you'd barely clear 20 centimeters.

Saturn has 95 times Earth's mass, yet you'd jump a bit higher there than at home.

And on Pluto, one push sends you nearly 8 meters up.

Same legs. Same jump. Gravity decides the rest.
