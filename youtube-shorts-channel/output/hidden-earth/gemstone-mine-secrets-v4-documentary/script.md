## Verbatim Final Spoken Narration

Movies picture gemstone mines as glittering crystal caves. In real underground mines, workers drill through solid grey rock.

Miners listen for the drill bit to suddenly drop into a hollow pocket. Inside, they don't find clean air—they hit sticky wet clay.

Millions of years ago, superheated mineral fluids cooled inside sealed granite cavities. Thick pocket clay cushioned razor-sharp emeralds, tourmaline, and fluorite as they grew.

Only after miners rinse away that grey mud do raw crystal faces flash with color. Yet above ground, jewelers slice out cloudy specks as worthless flaws.

To deep-Earth geologists, those rejected flaws are priceless. Some rough diamonds blast upward through volcanic pipes from more than six hundred kilometers down.

Firing X-ray beams into flawed diamonds, scientists found ringwoodite—a deep mineral locking away water. In twenty eighteen, diamonds even revealed Ice-Seven: solid high-pressure water ice.

That doesn't mean an open underground ocean sloshes beneath Earth's crust. Most of that deep water stays trapped inside crystal rock and microscopic fluid pockets.

Jewelers polish away tiny inclusions for sparkle. Scientists read those same crystal traps as time capsules—carried up through solid mine rock.

   - *Supports:* Discovery of terrestrial hydrous ringwoodite (~1.4–1.5 wt% water) sealed inside a super-deep rough diamond from Juína, Brazil, demonstrating water-rich regions in Earth's 410–660 km mantle transition zone.

   - *Supports:* Synchrotron X-ray diffraction identification of natural solid high-pressure cubic water ice (Ice-VII) preserved at gigapascal residual pressures inside deep-mantle diamonds from 410–800 km depth.
