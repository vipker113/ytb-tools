## YouTube Description (Ready to Copy-Paste — 3,840 chars, < 5,000 YouTube Limit, Full CC BY Credits)

Movies picture gemstone mines as open, glittering crystal caverns. Inside real underground hard-rock pegmatite and vein mines, miners drill through solid grey rock listening for the drill bit to drop into a sealed miarolitic cavity ("gem pocket")—only to find the pocket packed with thick, sticky hydrothermal clay rather than empty air.

Formed as volatile-rich granitic fluids cooled underground, that kaolinite- and smectite-rich pocket clay cushioned delicate emerald, tourmaline, quartz, and fluorite crystals as they grew (London, 2008). Only after miners rinse away the grey mud do sharp crystal faces flash with color—yet above ground, gem cutters slice away cloudy internal bubbles and dark mineral specks ("inclusions") as value-destroying flaws.

To deep-Earth geologists, those rejected inclusions are indestructible time capsules. Firing synchrotron X-ray beams into super-deep rough diamonds carried rapidly upward through volcanic kimberlite pipes from 410 to over 660 kilometers down, researchers discovered hydrous ringwoodite (~1.4 wt% water; Pearson et al., Nature, 2014) and natural cubic Ice-VII—a solid high-pressure phase of water ice sealed at gigapascal pressures (Tschauner et al., Science, 2018). While this does not mean a sloshing underground sea exists beneath Earth's crust, it proves Earth's deep mantle transition zone locks away vast reservoirs of water inside high-pressure crystal lattices and microscopic fluid pockets.
