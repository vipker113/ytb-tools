## Verbatim Final Spoken Narration

In Antarctica's freezing Dry Valleys, a five-story waterfall pours blood-red straight out of solid glacier ice.

For decades, nobody knew what fed Taylor Glacier's Blood Falls. Today, we know the color comes from chemistry: salty, iron-rich water hits the air and instantly rusts.

Deep under the ice sits a trapped reservoir of hyper-salty brine—likely ancient seawater sealed off as the glacier advanced, though its full history remains under investigation.

Rust explains the color. What surprised researchers in 2026 was what they pulled from the freezing red mud.

Testing 167 ice, sediment, air, and ocean samples across the region, a field team uncovered a hidden community of marine microbes right at the red outflow.

Under the microscope were tiny ocean algae like diatoms, alongside single-celled marine drifters—living clues of past ocean connections, miles from open sea.

Now, that doesn't prove these exact organisms stayed trapped beneath the ice for millions of years. Scientists are still piecing together how they got there.

Blood Falls isn't bleeding. It's a leaking subglacial vault—showing that even beneath 400 meters of Antarctic ice, Earth holds unexpected signs of life.
