## Verbatim Final Spoken Narration

Deep inside sealed volcanic lava tubes, NASA scientists shine their headlamps upward. Bare black rock suddenly glitters like solid gold.

Explorers are forbidden from touching it, because a single fingerprint destroys decades of growth. Up close, that metallic sheen contains zero gold.

It's a paper-thin mat of extremophile bacteria living in total darkness without sunlight. They pull chemical energy straight out of iron and manganese in rock.

To avoid drowning in cave moisture, the colony weaves a microscopic water-repellent fuzz. Millions of beaded droplets act like tiny lenses—bouncing headlamp beams back as gold.

Wearing sterile gloves, NASA teams scan these fragile walls with ultraviolet light and three-D LiDAR. They're training instruments to spot life without touching it.

Why practice underground? Radiation sterilizes Mars's open surface—yet in 2024, orbital radar confirmed an accessible lava-tube cave beneath the Moon. Deep volcanic pits dot Mars too.

Of course, finding rock-eating microbes in Earth's caves doesn't prove life exists inside Martian or lunar tunnels.

Cave gold isn't buried treasure. It's a living microbial reflector—turning dark volcanic rock into shelter for life.
