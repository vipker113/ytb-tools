## YouTube Description (Ready to Copy-Paste — Includes Required Media Credits)

Deep inside pitch-black volcanic lava tubes in Hawaiʻi and California's Lava Beds National Monument, headlamp beams transform bare black basalt ceilings into vaults that glitter like 24-karat solid gold.

Known colloquially as "cave gold," this metallic sheen contains zero gold metal—and touching it is strictly prohibited because a single fingerprint can destroy decades of fragile microbial growth. Up close, the golden crust is a paper-thin biofilm of chemolithotrophic bacteria (including Actinobacteria) surviving in total darkness without photosynthesis by oxidizing iron, manganese, and other minerals in volcanic rock (Northup et al., Astrobiology, 2011; Lavoie et al., PLOS ONE, 2017). Because these bacterial mats are hydrophobic (water-repellent), condensed cave moisture beads up into millions of microscopic spherical droplets that retro-reflect headlamp beams back to the viewer's eyes as a shimmering gold glow.

NASA astrobiologists in projects such as BRAILLE and BASALT use sterile sampling, UV fluorescence, Raman spectroscopy, and 3D LiDAR inside terrestrial lava tubes to develop non-contact life-detection protocols for future planetary missions. In July 2024, researchers re-analyzing NASA Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter radar data confirmed an accessible underground lava-tube cave conduit beneath the Mare Tranquillitatis pit on the Moon (Carrer et al., Nature Astronomy, 2024), complementing candidate lava-tube skylight pits imaged on Mars. While subterranean volcanic caves could shield potential biosignatures from harsh surface radiation, microbial life in Earth's lava tubes does not prove life exists inside Martian or lunar caves.
