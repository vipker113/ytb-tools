## Verbatim Final Spoken Narration

Two miles underwater, black chimneys blast three-hundred-sixty-degree water—without boiling into steam.

Along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, three hundred sixty atmospheres of crushing ocean weight trap superheated water as liquid. When robotic arms thrust a steel thermometer inside, that scalding fluid hits two-degree seawater—freezing dissolved metals into towering black chimneys.

You would expect bare, sterile rock around poison that hot. Instead, the chimney walls are moving.

Up to twenty-five hundred pale shrimp pack every square meter in total darkness—centimeters from water hot enough to melt lead. Look closely, and none of them have eyeballs.

Across their backs sits a white light-sensing organ that detects the faint infrared glow of scalding vents. It keeps them inside a narrow 20-degree safety zone—close enough to farm sulfur-eating bacteria in their gills without cooking alive.

Since 1977, researchers still debate whether Earth's first cells sparked in deep vents or shallow surface pools. Either way, these blind shrimp prove whole ecosystems can thrive without sunlight—making underwater chimneys a prime target in Europa's hidden ocean.

   - *Supports:* Rimicaris exoculata lacks normal image-forming eyes and possesses a reflective dorsal rhodopsin photoreceptor organ sensitive to faint thermal radiation from ~350°C vents.
2. **Petersen, J. M. et al. (2011). Hydrogen is an energy source for hydrothermal vent symbioses. Nature, 476, 176–180; Ponsard, J. et al. (2013). Inorganic carbon fixation by chemosynthetic ectosymbionts of Rimicaris exoculata. ISME Journal, 7, 96–109.** — https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10325
   - *Supports:* Chemosynthetic ectosymbiotic bacteria inside Rimicaris gill chambers oxidize hydrogen sulfide and reduced compounds to nourish dense (~2,500/m²) shrimp swarms without sunlight.
3. **Humphris, S. E. et al. (1995). The internal structure of an active sea-floor massive sulphide deposit (TAG hydrothermal mound, 3,670 m, Mid-Atlantic Ridge). Nature, 377, 713–716; Martin, W. et al. (2008). Hydrothermal vents and the origin of life. Nature Reviews Microbiology, 6, 805–814.** — https://doi.org/10.1038/377713a0
   - *Supports:* At ~3,600 m (~360 bar hydrostatic pressure), ~360°C hydrothermal fluids remain liquid without boiling, precipitate metal-sulfide chimneys upon mixing with ~2°C seawater, and serve as a leading (though debated vs. terrestrial hot springs) model for abiogenesis and icy-moon astrobiology.
