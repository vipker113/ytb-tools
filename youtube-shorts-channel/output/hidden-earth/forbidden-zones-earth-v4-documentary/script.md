## Verbatim Final Spoken Narration

Every forbidden zone on Earth looks like a warning built to save you. Step inside three locked-off places, and the real danger flips completely.

Around Chernobyl's shattered reactor, a giant exclusion zone still bars normal human life. Radiation there burned whole pine stands rust-red, creating what workers named the Red Forest.

Yet once people vanished, wildlife surged back. Gray wolves, wild horses, and deer multiplied across empty towns faster than in nearby reserves.

Even stranger, robots probing inside the reactor walls found pitch-black fungi growing toward radiation. Packed with dark melanin pigment, those fungi harvest lethal gamma rays for energy.

In southwestern France, Lascaux Cave is locked for the exact opposite reason. Humans aren't banned to save visitors—we are the poison.

For seventeen thousand years, prehistoric animal paintings survived inside cool, sealed limestone air. When crowds poured in, human breath, body heat, and shoe spores triggered white mold that ate the art. France sealed the real cave forever and built an exact copy nearby.

Off Iceland's coast, Surtsey Island bans humans for an even stricter purpose: pure creation. When an undersea volcano boiled up in nineteen sixty-three, scientists quarantined the barren rock immediately. Researchers scrub every seed from their boots—and when one stray tomato sprouted in ash, they ripped it out.

Carried only by ocean waves and nesting gulls, sixty plant species turned black lava green. That doesn't mean radiation is harmless, or that sealed caves ever recover once human crowds break their balance.

Some forbidden zones lock us out to keep our bodies alive. The rarest ones lock us out because a single human footprint changes everything.

   - *Supports:* Melanin-rich fungi including Cladosporium sphaerospermum isolated from Chernobyl reactor walls exhibit directional radiotropism and enhanced metabolic growth under ionizing gamma radiation.

   - *Supports:* Strict human quarantine on volcanic Surtsey Island since its 1963 submarine eruption, biosecurity protocols preventing anthropogenic seed introduction (including removal of an accidental human-introduced tomato plant), and seagull-driven nutrient enrichment enabling >60 vascular plant species to colonize barren basalt.
