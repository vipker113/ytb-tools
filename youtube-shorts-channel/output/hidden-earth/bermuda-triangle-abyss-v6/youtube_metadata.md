## YouTube Description (Ready to Copy-Paste — Includes Required Media Credits)

At the southern edge of the Bermuda Triangle, the Atlantic seafloor plunges more than 8,300 meters into the Puerto Rico Trench—the deepest point in the entire Atlantic Ocean.

Before modern multibeam sonar and deep-sea remotely operated vehicles (ROVs), ships lost over abyssal waters this deep simply vanished beyond human reach. Above the surface, where the warm, fast-moving Gulf Stream collides with North Atlantic storms, steep rogue waves up to 30 meters high can overwhelm steel vessels in minutes—including the cargo steamer SS Cotopaxi, lost in 1925 and identified underwater off Florida decades later.

While seafloor methane blowouts were once proposed as a ship-sinking mechanism, U.S. Geological Survey records show no large gas-hydrate releases in this region for roughly 15,000 years. Instead, seismologists analyzing earthquake waves beneath Bermuda uncovered a genuine geological anomaly: an unusual 20-kilometer-thick layer of frozen, lower-density mantle rock underplated beneath the oceanic crust—a hidden geological "raft" holding the Bermuda seafloor swell ~500 meters higher than normal. According to NOAA and U.S. Coast Guard records, vessels do not vanish here at higher rates than in any other heavily traveled, storm-prone ocean corridor.
