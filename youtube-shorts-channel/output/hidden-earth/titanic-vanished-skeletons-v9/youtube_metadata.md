## YouTube Description (Ready to Copy-Paste — Includes Required Media Credits)

Nearly four kilometers beneath the North Atlantic (~3,800 meters / 12,500 feet), robotic surveys of the RMS Titanic debris field photographed leather boots and shoes still resting in matched pairs where victims settled—yet across the entire exposed wreck and seafloor, cameras have never found a single human bone.

Deep-sea taphonomy explains this haunting contrast. Within weeks of sinking, abyssal scavengers strip exposed soft tissue. At 3,800 meters—under roughly 380 atmospheres of hydrostatic pressure in near-freezing (1–2°C), mineral-undersaturated deep water—exposed calcium-phosphate bone mineral (hydroxyapatite) is gradually dissolved by seawater chemistry and acid-secreting marine bone-degrading microbes and organisms over decades. By contrast, early-20th-century bootmakers cured cowhide in oak-bark tannic acid, chemically cross-linking collagen fibers into a rot-resistant polymer that marine bacteria cannot digest. Oceanographers note that sealed, oxygen-starved interior compartments deep inside Titanic's hull could theoretically still shelter isolated fragments from scavengers and circulating currents.
