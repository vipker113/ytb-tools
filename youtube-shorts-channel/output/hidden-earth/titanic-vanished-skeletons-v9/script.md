## Verbatim Final Spoken Narration

Four kilometers underwater, leather shoes still rest beside the Titanic—so where did every human skeleton go?

Across the North Atlantic seafloor at thirty-eight hundred meters, robotic surveys photographed boots lying in matched pairs where victims settled. Yet across the entire exposed wreck and debris field, cameras have never found a single human bone.

Deep-sea scavengers strip soft tissue within weeks, leaving bare skeletons exposed to freezing water under three hundred eighty atmospheres of pressure. At this mineral-starved depth, seawater and acid-secreting marine microbes slowly dissolve the calcium-phosphate crystals inside bone until nothing remains.

Early twentieth-century bootmakers, however, soaked cowhide in oak-bark tannic acid, locking collagen fibers into a rot-proof polymer. Deep-sea bacteria cannot digest tanned leather, allowing century-old boots to outlast both flesh and solid bone.

Oceanographers note that sealed, oxygen-starved rooms deep inside Titanic's hull could still shelter isolated fragments from scavengers and currents. Out on the open mud, only empty pairs of shoes remain—silent footprints where passengers came to rest.

## Factual Sources Supporting the Narration

1. **NOAA Ocean Exploration & Robert Ballard / Institute for Exploration (2004). Return to Titanic Expedition (June 2004, NOAA Ship Ronald H. Brown & ROV Hercules, 3,800 m North Atlantic).** — https://archive.oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/explorations/04titanic/welcome.html
   - *Supports:* RMS Titanic rests ~3,800 meters (~4 km / 12,500 ft) beneath the North Atlantic under ~380 atmospheres of hydrostatic pressure in 1–2°C deep water; ROV surveys documented tanned leather boots and shoes lying in matched pairs on the debris-field sediment where victims settled, while zero exposed human bones have ever been observed across the exterior wreck or debris field (with oceanographers noting sealed, anoxic interior hull spaces could theoretically shelter isolated fragments).
2. **Tresguerres, M., Katz, S., & Rouse, G. W. (2013). How to get into bones: proton pump and carbonic anhydrase in Osedax boneworms. Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 280(1761), 20130625; paired with Smith, C. R., & Baco, A. R. (2003). Ecology of whale falls at the deep-sea floor. Oceanography and Marine Biology: An Annual Review, 41, 311–354, and Covington, A. D. (2009). Tanning Chemistry: The Science of Leather (RSC Publishing).** — https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2013.0625
   - *Supports:* Deep-sea scavengers strip vertebrate soft tissue within weeks; exposed hydroxyapatite (calcium-phosphate) bone matrix in cold, mineral-undersaturated abyssal seawater is progressively dissolved over ~5–30 years by seawater undersaturation and acid-secreting marine microbes/bone-degrading organisms, whereas early-20th-century vegetable tanning with oak-bark tannic acid cross-links hide collagen into a bacteria-resistant polymer capable of enduring over 110 years on the seafloor.
