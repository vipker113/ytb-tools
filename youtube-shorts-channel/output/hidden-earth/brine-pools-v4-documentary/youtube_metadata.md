## YouTube Description (Ready to Copy-Paste — Includes Required Media Credits)

More than a kilometer down on the pitch-black ocean floor, deep-sea ROVs cross an eerie shoreline—into an underwater lake complete with rolling ripples and dark seabed streams.

Known as deep-sea brine pools, these basins form where buried ancient salt layers beneath regions like the Gulf of Mexico and the Red Sea dissolve into hypersaline water up to eight times saltier than normal seawater. Too dense to mix with overlying ocean water, the brine settles into seafloor depressions behind a razor-sharp density interface, trapping methane and sulfur compounds with zero dissolved oxygen.

While crabs or fish that stray into the anoxic brine can be rapidly stunned, dense beds of chemosynthetic mussels and tubeworms thrive along the rim without sunlight. In 2022, researchers exploring 1,770 meters down in the Red Sea's Gulf of Aqaba recovered intact sediment cores from undisturbed brine pools preserving over 1,000 years of regional earthquakes, flash floods, and tsunamis (Purkis et al., Communications Earth & Environment, 2022). Scientists also study these extreme chemical interfaces as Earth analogs for ocean worlds such as Europa—though that does not prove life exists beyond Earth.
